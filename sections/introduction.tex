\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

People spend much of their working day in front of a screen, and much of
what passes over it they will need again: the price cap they set while
shopping, the command they ran right after checking their IP address, the
album page they opened to copy a track list. Human memory is a poor index
for this stream. Most such details are seen once, briefly, and are gone by
the time they are needed~\needcite{human memory / re-finding studies}, and
recovering them by hand means guessing the right application, tab, or search
query~\cite{hu2020screentrack}. \emph{Episodic recall} over one's own laptop
activity, i.e.\ retrieving the moment on screen that answers a natural-language
question about the past, is therefore valuable to people, and increasingly to
computer-use agents that act on a person's behalf and must recall what that
person did before~\cite{yang2026personatrail}.

Screen-recall systems capture a screenshot every few seconds, or whenever
the screen changes, and index it. Existing systems rely heavily on OCR text.
Open-source tools such as Screenpipe, OpenRecall, and Rem index only the text
extracted by OCR or the accessibility tree, as did the now-discontinued
Rewind~\needcite{Screenpipe, OpenRecall, Rem, Rewind}. Microsoft Recall also
indexes screenshots, but it is closed source and requires a dedicated
40-TOPS NPU~\needcite{Microsoft Recall}. Research systems that go beyond text
send every screenshot to a cloud VLM for summarization~\cite{wang2025mirix},
so the screen history leaves the device. A screen history holds a person's
messages, documents, and account details, so it should be indexed on the
device, and there OCR is attractive because text is cheap to extract, store,
and embed. But OCR discards everything on the screen that is not text, such
as layout, icons, images, and the visual state of a form or page, and its
errors on small or stylized text propagate into the index.

Visual document retrieval has shown that embedding page images directly with
a vision-language model (VLM) can outperform OCR-then-embed pipelines on
static document collections~\cite{ma2024dse,faysse2025colpali}. We find that
the same holds on real screen streams. Holding the embedding model fixed
(Qwen3-VL-Embedding-2B~\needcite{Qwen3-VL-Embedding}) and indexing either each
screenshot or its OCR text, on 63,538 screenshots of one person's real
computer use with 5,507 time-split test queries, screenshots reach 65.6 R@1
zero-shot versus 59.5 for OCR text, and 68.6/85.8 versus 66.1/79.4 R@1/R@10
after identical LoRA fine-tuning. Screenshots, however, are slow to encode:
every frame is a full VLM forward pass over hundreds of visual tokens. On a
laptop (Apple M2 Pro), embedding one screenshot takes 2.86\,s on the GPU
versus 1.27\,s for the OCR route, and 6.61\,s versus 2.44\,s on the CPU.
Because a recall system captures 720 frames per screen-hour and must index
every one of them for as long as it runs, this gap is a permanent tax: at
2.86\,s per frame, indexing alone occupies the laptop GPU for 34 minutes of
every hour of screen time.

Our key observation is that most of this computation is redundant, in two
ways. \emph{Spatial redundancy}: much of a screen is blank or flat
(backgrounds, margins, whitespace). \emph{Temporal redundancy}: consecutive
frames, taken 5\,s apart, are often identical, and when they are not, only
small regions change (typing, a moving cursor). We remove each with a
matching technique. \emph{Token pruning} removes spatial redundancy within a
frame: following PixelPrune~\needcite{PixelPrune}, we drop every
$32{\times}32$ block whose pixels are predictable from its left and upper
neighbors (maximum pixel difference ${\le}\,8/255$) before it enters the ViT.
\emph{K/V reuse} removes temporal redundancy across frames: a block counts as
changed if any of its pixels differs by more than $8/255$ from when it was
last computed; the ViT caches every layer's keys and values and recomputes
only the changed patches, and the LLM reuses the cached keys and values of
unchanged visual tokens. Both techniques are training-free and apply to the
same embedding model at the original screen resolution.

Together, the two techniques cut compute from 6.17 to 1.93\,TFLOP per frame
(0.31$\times$) and GPU latency from 2.86 to 0.98\,s (0.34$\times$), which
makes the screenshot route faster than the OCR route on the same laptop
(0.98 vs.\ 1.27\,s on the GPU, 1.50 vs.\ 2.44\,s on the CPU). The screenshot
route still spends 1.6$\times$ the FLOPs of the OCR route; OCR is cheap in
FLOPs but slow in practice, because text extraction runs on the CPU as many
small, irregular operations. The accuracy cost is small. With LoRA, the
techniques cost at most 0.5 R@1 and 1.9 R@10, and the screenshot index still
leads OCR text by $+2.0$ R@1 and $+4.5$ R@10, with 95\% confidence intervals
that exclude zero. Zero-shot, token pruning costs 2.4 R@1, a loss that
fine-tuning absorbs.

Cheaper encoding changes what a recall system can afford. A lower cost per
frame makes larger pools, i.e.\ longer histories and denser capture,
affordable. This matters because a real history keeps growing, and retrieval
gets harder as it does: in a pilot study with 500 fixed queries, screenshot
R@1 fell from 78.8 to 53.2 as the pool grew from 5K to 80K frames, while
still leading OCR text at every pool size

In summary, we make the following contributions:
\begin{itemize}
\item We show on real human screen streams that, with the embedding model
  and training recipe held fixed, indexing screenshots retrieves
  significantly better than indexing their OCR text, but costs
  2.3$\times$ (GPU) to 2.7$\times$ (CPU) more time per frame on a laptop.
\item We identify spatial and temporal redundancy in screen streams and
  remove them without training, with token pruning within frames and K/V
  reuse across frames in both the ViT and the LLM. This cuts compute to
  0.31$\times$ and latency to 0.34$\times$ of the full screenshot, making
  the screenshot route faster than OCR text on a laptop while it keeps a
  $+2.0$ R@1 / $+4.5$ R@10 lead.
\item We build a time-split episodic-recall benchmark from human-annotated
  screen recordings of two people (PSAI-A: 117 days, 91.8 hours;
  PSAI-B: 57 days, 17.2 hours) and from computer-use
  rollouts (MyPCBench~\cite{jang2026mypcbench}), with single-hop, multi-hop,
  and agentic (recall-then-act) queries, where every query is checked
  against the person's entire history so that all matching recordings count
