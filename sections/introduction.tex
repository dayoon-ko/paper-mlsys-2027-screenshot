\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

In 1945, Vannevar Bush imagined the memex, a desk in which a person stores
all of their books, records, and communications and consults them with speed
and flexibility~\cite{bush1945memex}. Today's laptops record far more than
Bush imagined, but most of it is seen once and then forgotten. Weeks later,
a person asks: ``How did I set only one section of that document to
landscape?'' or ``What rent cap did I use when I searched for apartments?''
Screen-recall systems are built for such questions: they capture a
screenshot every few seconds and let the person search this
history~\cite{hu2020screentrack}. We call this task \emph{episodic recall}:
given a question about the past, return the screenshots that answer it.

Existing screen-recall systems store screenshots but search only the text on
them. Screenpipe, OpenRecall, and Rem index text from OCR or the
accessibility tree, as did Rewind~\needcite{Screenpipe, OpenRecall, Rem,
Rewind}; Microsoft Recall also indexes screenshots, but it is closed source
and requires a 40-TOPS NPU~\needcite{Microsoft Recall}. Text indexing suits
the privacy needs of this task. A screen history records sensitive
information, such as the two medications a person checked against each other
or the taxable income they entered into a tax calculator, so it should stay
on the device, where OCR text is cheap to extract, store, and embed. Systems
that send every screenshot to a cloud VLM~\cite{wang2025mirix} do not meet
this requirement. However, OCR text keeps the words on a screen and drops
the rest: the layout, the icons, and the state of menus and dialogs. The
answer to ``how did I set one section to landscape?'' lies in exactly such
menus and dialogs. Is the text on a screen enough to find it again, or should
the system search the screenshot itself?

\textbf{Screenshots versus OCR text: accuracy and cost.}
In this paper, we first show that searching the screenshot finds the right
moment more often than searching its text. We hold the embedding model fixed
(Qwen3-VL-Embedding-2B~\needcite{Qwen3-VL-Embedding}) and build both indexes
over 63,538 screenshots from one person's computer use, with 5,507 test
questions about later periods. Without training, the screenshot index
outperforms the OCR-text index by 6.1 points in R@1 (65.6\% vs.\ 59.5\%).
After the same LoRA fine-tuning, Table~\ref{tab:intro} shows a gap of 2.5
points in R@1 and 6.4 points in R@10. This extends the finding of visual
document retrieval~\cite{ma2024dse,faysse2025colpali} from static documents
to screen histories. The screenshot, however, is expensive to encode.
Table~\ref{tab:intro} also shows that encoding one screenshot takes 2.86\,s
on a laptop GPU, compared with 1.27\,s for OCR and text embedding. A recall
system encodes every screenshot it captures, so the GPU spends 34 minutes of
every hour of screen use on indexing, more than twice the 15 minutes of the
OCR-text index. Must a laptop then choose between the accuracy of
screenshots and the cost of text?

To answer this question, we look at what the encoder spends its compute on.
Most of each screenshot repeats what the encoder has already seen. Within a
screenshot, large areas such as backgrounds, margins, and whitespace are
blank or flat, so each of their blocks can be predicted from its neighbors
(spatial redundancy). Across screenshots taken 5\,s apart, most of the
screen does not change, and when it does, only small regions such as typed
text or the cursor differ (temporal redundancy). A screenshot index that
encodes every pixel of every screenshot pays for this repetition in full.
So, if most of a screenshot repeats its neighbors or the previous
screenshot, how much of the encoding can we skip without losing the
accuracy of screenshots?

\textbf{Our solution: Ditto.}
We present Ditto, a screenshot index that encodes only what does not repeat.
The name comes from the ditto mark, which means ``the same as above'': Ditto does not encode a block that repeats its neighbors or the
same block in the previous screenshot. Ditto removes spatial redundancy with
token pruning~\needcite{PixelPrune}, which drops each $32{\times}32$ block
whose pixels can be predicted from its left and upper neighbors before the
block enters the vision encoder. It removes temporal redundancy with K/V
reuse: a block counts as changed if any of its pixels differs by more than
$8/255$ from when it was last encoded, the vision encoder recomputes keys
and values only for changed blocks, and the language model reuses the
cached keys and values of unchanged visual tokens. Reused keys and values
can differ from recomputed ones, so Ditto encodes the whole screenshot again
when more than 60\% of its blocks change or every 10 screenshots. Neither
technique requires training.

We implement Ditto inside Qwen3-VL-Embedding-2B and run it on the GPU of an
Apple M2 Pro laptop. Table~\ref{tab:intro} shows that Ditto reduces encoding
compute to 31\% of the full screenshot and encoding time to 0.98\,s per
screenshot, below the 1.27\,s of the OCR-text index; indexing then takes 12
GPU minutes per hour of screen use instead of 34. The OCR-text index still
uses fewer FLOPs (1.22 vs.\ 1.93 TFLOP); its text extraction runs on the
CPU, so it is slower despite needing less computation. Ditto lowers R@1 by
at most 0.5 points and R@10 by at most 1.9 points, and it still outperforms
the OCR-text index by 2.0 points in R@1 and 4.5 points in R@10, with 95\%
confidence intervals that exclude zero. On a laptop GPU, then, the choice
between screenshots and text is not forced: screenshots can be encoded
faster than OCR text while keeping their accuracy advantage.

Lower encoding cost also changes what a recall system can do. First, a
person's history keeps growing, and recall gets harder as it does: in a
pilot study with 500 fixed questions, R@1 of the screenshot index drops from
78.8\% to 53.2\% as the pool grows from 5K to 80K screenshots, while staying
ahead of the OCR-text index at every pool size. Ditto makes larger pools
affordable to index \todo{larger-pool results in the main setting}. Second,
because Ditto returns screenshots rather than extracted text, a computer-use
agent can use it to recall a value from the person's past and finish a
task~\cite{yang2026personatrail}, such as finding a yoga mat with the same
filters the person used in August \todo{agentic results}. Thus, the screenshot itself, not its text, can serve
as the record that a personal memory system stores and searches, at a cost
a laptop can sustain.

To summarize, we make the following contributions:
\begin{itemize}
\item We show that, with the same embedding model and training, searching
  screenshots retrieves better than searching their OCR text on real screen
  histories, but takes 2.3$\times$ longer to encode on a laptop GPU.
\item We present Ditto, which removes spatial and temporal redundancy in
  screen streams with token pruning and K/V reuse, without training. Ditto
  encodes a screenshot faster than the OCR-text index on a laptop GPU and
  keeps a lead of 2.0 points in R@1 and 4.5 points in R@10.
\item We build an episodic-recall benchmark from the screen recordings of two
  people (PSAI-A: 117 days, 91.8 hours; PSAI-B: 57 days, 17.2 hours) and from
  computer-use rollouts (MyPCBench~\cite{jang2026mypcbench}), with
  single-hop, multi-hop, and agentic questions. Each question is checked
  against the person's whole history, so every recording that answers it
  counts as gold \todo{final question counts}.
\end{itemize}
